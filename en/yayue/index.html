Yayue, Court Ritual Music

Yayue is court ritual music for sacrifice and audience, shaped by occasion and rank rather than concert listening

When and where

The idea of yayue took shape in the Western Zhou, and its institutions are set out in the Zhouli, in the office of the Grand Music Master. Every later court maintained ritual music; the Qing called it zhonghe shaoyue and used it for sacrifices to Heaven, Confucius and the ancestors, and for court audiences. In 1914 the Beijing Confucian temple revived the sacrifice with old music. Courts in Japan, Korea and Vietnam also inherited Chinese yayue, each developing independently.

How it came about

The Zhouli says the Grand Music Master taught the six dynastic dances: Yunmen, Xianchi, Dashao, Daxia, Dahu and Dawu. Ensembles were graded by rank: the king had bells and chimes on four sides, feudal lords on three, high ministers on two, officers on one; dance rows were similarly fixed. Later dynasties re-established ritual music again and again. Sui-Tang yayue, the Song Dasheng music and the Qing zhonghe shaoyue were all scholarly reconstructions.

The sound's frame

Yayue sets one syllable to one note, moves slowly and keeps melodic contour narrow; its purpose is to frame an occasion. Bells and lithophones form the frame, joined by sheng, guan, panpipes, xun, drums, the zhu and the yu. Singing is mostly pentatonic, sometimes heptatonic. The music avoids variation and builds gravity through timbre, volume and formation. The scoring for sacrifice, audience and banquet differs.

What to listen for

Hear the Sacrificial Song for long repeated phrases and sinking endings, the pace of ritual singing. Then hear the Qing court Qilong Wu song, a banquet dance piece rather than sacrificial yayue, to compare another court use. Finally hear the piano piece titled Yayue E Jue Mode; the composer borrows a lu name, not the sound of yayue.

What is often mistaken

The common mistake is to take yayue for the whole of ancient Chinese music, or to assume it never changed. It was one ritual layer, with folk song, opera and instrumental music growing beside it; every dynasty rechecked the old models, and what survives is mostly Ming-Qing practice or modern reconstruction. Yayue was not uniquely Chinese either; East Asian courts each had their own version.
