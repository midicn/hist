The Cello Sonata Turns

Berlin in 1796, when Beethoven moved the cello out of the bass and let it sing

2 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-30

music historycelloBeethovenchamber music

Related

ClassicalCelloSonataPiano

In 1796 Beethoven wrote two sonatas in Berlin for the Prussian court cellist Jean-Louis Duport. The cello moved from the floor of the continuo to a voice as articulate as the violin.

The year, the place

Berlin and Vienna, 1796. Beethoven wrote the two sonatas of Opus 5 for Duport, with the cello stating the melody at the start. Earlier cello sonatas were mostly cello with continuo, a keyboard or another bass instrument filling the harmony while the cello stayed in its lower range.

The event itself

Opus 5 puts both instruments on one level: the piano no longer merely accompanies, and the cello carries themes and counterpoint in high and low registers alike. In the Third and Fourth Sonatas Beethoven pushed the cello toward a more singing, more dramatic role, and the opening monologue of the Fourth is close to recitative. The change was gradual. Keyboard and cello sonatas existed by the mid-eighteenth century, but the cello was usually written as a bass. Beethoven took it out of the bass and let it sing in the middle and upper register, competing with the piano for the theme.

What it left behind

The cello's solo repertory separated from teaching and accompaniment and became concert music. Players needed new technique, thumb position, singing in the high register and breathing with the piano, all written into the score. Publishers issued sonatas for piano and cello, and the chamber market gained a new pairing. Brahms, Grieg and Rachmaninoff later worked along the same line.

Listen for the turn

Begin with the opening of the Third Sonata, the cello singing first and the piano answering. Then its slow movement, where the two exchange the same motive. Finally the first movement of Brahms's First Sonata, the cello's low register pressed by dense piano harmony. Opus 5 is not in the catalogue; these three works show the later half of the road.
