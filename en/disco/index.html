Disco

Four-on-the-floor kick, offbeat hi-hat and strings turn the dance floor into a machine that keeps lifting.

1 min readGenres and LineageUpdated 2026-10-28

discodancefour-on-the-floor1970s

Disco uses a kick on every beat, open hi-hat on the offbeats and strings to turn the dance floor into a machine that keeps lifting.

Era and place

In the early 1970s New York and Philadelphia clubs stitched soul, funk and Latin rhythm into continuous dance music. In 1977 Saturday Night Fever took disco global. After the 1979 backlash, its four-beat pulse and synth bass entered house.

What you hear

Start with the kick, landing on all four beats, with open hi-hat on the offbeats like a metronome. Bass runs eighth notes or syncopated figures, guitar chops offbeat chords, and strings and brass stretch long lines above. Tempos sit mostly between 110 and 130 BPM, and sections rise through drum fills and harmonic lift rather than verse-chorus contrast.

Key figures

Donna Summer joined long dance tracks to a powerful voice, and the Bee Gees wrote film-style disco with falsetto and strings. Giorgio Moroder was key to production and synthesizers, while Chic's Nile Rodgers brought funk guitar to the floor.

Lines before and after

Soul, funk and Philadelphia soul feed in; house, synth-pop and dance-pop come out. Its four-beat bass also enters later electronic dance music.

Common misunderstandings

Disco is not just old dance music; its arranging and production shaped house directly. Nor is it shallow, since long tracks work by layered arrangement. Treating it as costume and era misses the engineering of drums and bass.
