Baojuan, Precious Scrolls

Baojuan are Ming-Qing scroll texts of sectarian and Buddhist ritual, alternating prose and verse and performed in xuanjuan

2 min readChinese MusicUpdated 2026-10-30

baojuanxuanjuansectarian religionstorytelling

Time and place

Baojuan appeared in quantity from the sixteenth century, circulating in the Hexi corridor of Gansu, Jingjiang in Jiangsu, Hebei and around Lake Tai. They grew from Tang popular lectures, Song sutra storytelling and repentance rites, and Ming-Qing sectarian groups used new scrolls to spread their teachings. Xuanjuan performance took place at temple fairs, household rites and sect gatherings; it declined after the mid-twentieth century and is now recorded as heritage.

What happened

Lu Qing compiled the Wubu Liuce in the sixteenth century, supplying scriptures for the Wuwei sect and fixing the alternation of prose and verse that defines baojuan. Later groups wrote new scrolls, expanding from Buddhist figures to folk legend, moral instruction and historical romance. Performers were often semi-professional masters who sang and recited from the scroll while listeners answered the Buddha name.

Musical features

Prose carries the plot and verse carries the song. Verses run mostly in seven- or ten-character lines rhymed in local speech. The music has layers: Buddha invocations, named qupai and local tunes, with wooden fish, bowl gong, bells and drum board marking time. The same scroll may be sung to different regional melodies, so baojuan is both a textual and a local musical tradition.

What to listen for

Hear Cuilian Juan, a long verse narrative moving episode by episode, for the pace of scroll storytelling. Then hear Jing Yun, a steady Buddhist ritual melody that feeds baojuan verse. Then hear Nianfo Diao, short repeated invocations often placed after prose. Finally hear Dan Jing Diao, sung on pilgrimage, close to the ritual space of xuanjuan. None is a confirmed baojuan performance.

Common misunderstandings

Baojuan are often taken for Buddhist scriptures. They are performance texts used by sectarian and Buddhist groups, drawing on Buddhist material but also on folk tales and moral books. Others assume they are read, not sung; in xuanjuan singing dominates and only the prose approaches speech. Nor are they surviving Tang transformation texts; centuries of change separate the two.
