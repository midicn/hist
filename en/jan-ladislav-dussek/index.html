Jan Ladislav Dussek

A pianist from Bohemia to Paris who taught the piano to sing and to act

2 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-30

composerclassicalbohemiapiano

Training and teachers

Jan Ladislav Dussek (1760–1812) was born in Čáslav, Bohemia, and as a young man worked between Prague and Amsterdam before playing in The Hague and London in the 1780s. In 1789 he settled in London, married the singer Sophia Corri, and joined her family's music-publishing business. The firm failed in 1800; he left Britain, went first to Hamburg, then into the circle of Prince Louis Ferdinand of Prussia. The prince was killed in 1806, and Dussek returned to Paris in 1807, dying at Saint-Germain-en-Laye in 1812.

His place

Ten years older than Beethoven, he belongs to the high Classical period yet already wrote dramatic, programmatic piano music. He favoured the wide range and full bass of English Broadwood pianos, and his melodies often sit in the middle register, asking the key to sustain like a voice. His sonatas were published in London and Paris and appeared on pianists' programmes; today only a few are played, and most of the rest survive on recordings. His music sits between Haydn's phrasing and Weber's drama, a useful sample of a style in transition.

Where to start

Listen first for the singing right hand: phrases often begin off the beat, and the notes are joined by touch and dynamics rather than by pedal alone. Then for sudden modulation: at the end of a paragraph he will push the harmony far away, lose the listener's bearings, and then settle back. Next, the tread of the bass: the left hand walks in octaves or broken chords and supports the freedom above. The three sonatas of Op. 39 let you compare how he changes mood within one genre. Finally, the narrative hints in Op. 23, where bells, marches and weeping figures are written into the keyboard — an early attempt at storytelling on the piano.

Works and craft

  • Piano sonatas Op. 9, Op. 39, Op. 70 "Le retour à Paris"
  • The Sufferings of the Queen of France, Op. 23
  • Piano concertos, chamber music and harp music

Influence and misreadings

Dussek's singing touch shaped later pianists, and his sonatas gave Beethoven's generation a model to argue with. His narrative writing was taken up by nineteenth-century composers, though his name is often forgotten. He turned the piano from a clear keyboard into an instrument that could speak and act.
