Britpop

1960s melody, regional accents and singalong choruses in mid-1990s Britain.

2 min readGenres and LineageUpdated 2026-10-30

Britpop brought the melody of 1960s British guitar pop, observational lyrics and regional accents back to the charts, forming in London between 1992 and 1997.

Where it came from

In 1992 Suede's single The Drowners set the press talking about a new wave of British guitar bands. Blur's Modern Life Is Rubbish in 1993, Parklife in 1994 and Oasis's Definitely Maybe pushed the scene forward, with Pulp's Different Class and Elastica following. In August 1995 Blur and Oasis released singles in the same week, and the press turned it into a contest whose story travelled further than the music.

What the ear catches

Tempos usually run from 110 to 140 BPM, drums play a standard two and four, and guitars use clean or lightly distorted chords and short phrases. Melody leans openly on the 1960s, with observational lyrics, rising harmony, music-hall piano and brass all appearing. The singer keeps a regional accent, choruses are built for group singing, songs last three to four minutes, and bridges often shift key or speed up. A rising bass phrase often leads into the chorus, where cymbals and handclaps build the sense of a crowd. Lyrics name streets, bus routes and newspaper headlines, and that detail separates it from American rock of the same years.

Names you cannot avoid

Damon Albarn and Graham Coxon of Blur wrote British domestic detail into songs, while Noel Gallagher of Oasis used simple chords and high choruses to make stadium singalongs. Jarvis Cocker of Pulp brought narrative and satire, Brett Anderson of Suede supplied a more theatrical image, and Elastica restored post-punk tightness.

What it influenced

1960s British guitar pop, 1970s glam rock and punk feed in; 2000s indie rock and the British guitar revival come out. It made Britishness a subject of pop again, from clothing and accents to city landmarks used in promotion. After 1997 the scene faded with the press cycle, and bands moved toward wider pop or split. Its influence survives more in attitude and promotion than in any fixed sound.

What is often misread

The common claim is that Britpop was one unified movement, when the bands differed widely in style and class background and many rejected the label. Another is that it ran on nostalgia alone; electronic and dance influences were just as visible, and drum machines and samples never left British pop of the period. Treating it as a purely musical revival misses the role of the press and the labels.
