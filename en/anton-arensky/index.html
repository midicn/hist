Anton Arensky

A Tchaikovsky disciple who wrote salon miniatures and strict counterpoint with equal polish

A life in brief

Anton Arensky (1861–1906) was born in Novgorod and died at Terijoki in Finland, today Zelenogorsk. He studied composition with Rimsky-Korsakov at the St Petersburg Conservatory, then taught harmony and counterpoint at the Moscow Conservatory from 1882; Rachmaninoff and Scriabin both passed through his classes. In 1895 he returned to St Petersburg to direct the Imperial Court Choir, leaving that post in 1901. His health declined and he died of tuberculosis in 1906.

The city he worked in

He stands between Tchaikovsky and the Russian academic school. In Moscow he wrote chamber music and piano miniatures, clean in workmanship and easy to remember, suited to the salon and the teaching studio; in St Petersburg he turned to choral and church music. He joined neither side of the nationalist argument and chased no novelty. His music was popular in his lifetime and faded quickly after his death, leaving only the Variations on a Theme of Tchaikovsky and the First Piano Trio in circulation. The drop has to do with method: he never pushes his material to its limit and prefers a piece short and smooth to one that strains.

What to hear

Listen first for the breathing of a canon. The six pieces of Op.1 are student work, and the entries follow each other closely; hear how the second voice changes the harmony without altering the theme. Then the label of character. Each of the twenty-four pieces of Op.36 fixes its identity with one rhythm or one kind of touch, and each is short with little repetition, so notice how detail separates pieces of the same genre. Then the late tightening. The preludes of Op.63 are shorter than the early music and more restrained in harmony; listen for phrases that stop halfway. Finally the division of labour. In Op.53 the right hand sings while the left supplies only bass and inner voice, and it never competes with the tune.

Key works

  • Piano: 24 Morceaux caractéristiques Op.36, Preludes Op.63, Etudes Op.74
  • Chamber: Piano Trio No.1 Op.32, String Quartet No.2 Op.35
  • Orchestral: Variations on a Theme of Tchaikovsky Op.35a, the ballet Egyptian Nights
  • Two pianos: four suites

What remains

His influence came mainly through teaching. The harmony and counterpoint courses at the Moscow Conservatory took shape under him, and Rachmaninoff and Scriabin carried that training into their own works. The Tchaikovsky Variations turned a memorial into a set of variations, and the piece became standard repertoire for Russian string orchestras; the elegiac tone of the First Trio was heard as the close of Tchaikovsky's generation. The twentieth century rated him low, mostly because the music is agreeable rather than because the craft is thin.
