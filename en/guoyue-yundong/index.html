The National Music Reform Movement

The national music reform was a 1920s attempt by Liu Tianhua and others to remake Chinese instrumental music with new notation and technique

1 min readChinese MusicUpdated 2026-10-30

Time and place

The national music reform ran through the 1920s and 1930s, centred on Beijing and Shanghai. In 1922 Peking University's music institute opened with Liu Tianhua on the faculty; in 1927 he and colleagues founded the Guoyue Gaijin She in Beijing and launched its Music Magazine in 1928. In Shanghai the Datong Music Society, founded in 1920, took a different path through ensemble arrangement.

What happened

After the May Fourth movement, musicians debated how Chinese music should modernise. Liu Tianhua argued for taking from both sides: keep the timbre and inflection of erhu and pipa, but adopt staff notation, metre marks, études and the solo recital. The society also organised concerts and lectures to win an audience. He composed Bing Zhong Yin, Liangxiao and Guangming Xing, lifting the erhu from accompaniment to solo status, and wrote new works for pipa.

Musical features

Reform works keep a pentatonic frame but square off the phrasing and mark tempo, dynamics, bowing and fingering in detail. The erhu gains position shifts, vibrato and fast bowing, widening its range; the pipa takes on new chordal and tremolo patterns. Fingering charts and graded studies made the new technique teachable. Accompaniment may be piano or a small ensemble, with a texture closer to Western chamber music than to traditional silk-and-bamboo.

What to listen for

Hear Moli Hua, whose pentatonic frame and phrase endings made it a favourite source. Then hear the piano piece Jasmine Blossoms, the same melody rewritten for a Western instrument, to compare two approaches. Finally hear the modern piano work titled with a Chinese lu name, a different route to Sino-Western synthesis. None is an erhu or reform recording.

Common misunderstandings

The reform is often called wholesale Westernisation. Liu Tianhua repeatedly stressed keeping Chinese timbre and breath; what he changed was notation, teaching and concert practice, not turning the erhu into a violin. Others think it only reformed instruments, though composition, education, journals and concerts mattered equally. Nor was it the work of one man.
