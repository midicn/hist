The Suona Tradition

A double reed in a wooden cone with a brass bell, the suona leads wind-and-percussion bands at weddings, funerals and temple fairs

Related

SuonaQupai

Time and place

The suona arrived from Central Asia along the Silk Road around the thirteenth century; Ming sources already write it as suona or suernai. It settled on two tracks: village wind-and-percussion bands in the north, and opera stages and gong teams along the southeast coast. Heze and Jining in Shandong, Kaifeng in Henan, Dingzhou in Hebei, Wutai in Shanxi and the Suide-Mizhi belt in northern Shaanxi all keep dense networks of bands.

What happened

Open-air occasions needed a melodic instrument that could cut through gongs, drums and voices, and a double reed in a wooden cone with a brass bell did exactly that. At temple fairs, weddings, funerals and opera stages the suona set the pitch for everyone. In the north it joined sheng, dizi, drum and cymbals in guchui and chuida suites; in the south it entered Chaozhou drum music, Hakka zhongjun bands and Cantonese opera. After the 1950s pieces such as Birds Worshipping the Phoenix were arranged as solos, taking the instrument from ritual grounds into concert halls, conservatories and the modern orchestra.

Musical features

The body is a conical wooden tube with eight finger holes, seven in front and one behind, a flattened reed at the top and a brass bell below. The small suona in D is piercing; the large one sounds an octave lower, and Chaozhou dachui uses an even bigger instrument. The working range is about two octaves, with the fundamental treated as sol or do and fingerings changed to suit the key. Players use circular breathing, single, double and triple tonguing, flutter tonguing, slides, trills and grace notes, plus a technique that makes the reed and throat imitate speech or opera arias.

What to listen for

Hear the nasal edge of the reed and the brightness of the bell, then the grain of the tonguing. Northern guchui phrases are long, held through circular breathing; opera suonas play short labelled tunes that lock into the percussion. The library's Sipan from Rizhao, Kan Shehuo from Lushi and the Shaanxi Single Suona Work Song are folk songs and work songs, not suona recordings, but they carry the speech rhythm and occasion of the regions where the instrument rules.

Common misunderstandings

A common mistake is to hear the suona as mere noise for weddings and funerals. In opera it must support the voice and follow the drummer's metre, and its intonation and breath control are exacting; the bird calls in Birds Worshipping the Phoenix depend on tongue and reed control, not on blowing harder. Another mistake is to treat all high suonas as one instrument: the small suona, the large suona and the Chaozhou dachui differ in register and function.
