Prokofiev

Modernist who leapt between sarcasm and lyricism, reshaping Russian music through rhythm

1 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

composermodernismrussiapianoballet

Life

Sergei Prokofiev (1891–1953) was a Russian and Soviet composer and pianist trained at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory. He burst out as a musical futurist, lived in the West after the Revolution, returned to the USSR in 1936, and spent his last years in a complicated relationship with the Soviet state. He was both an international modernist and an officially recognised Soviet writer of symphonies and operas.

His place

He is the most theatrical of the twentieth-century modernists: the Classical Symphony looks back to Haydn, Romeo and Juliet is ballet, Alexander Nevsky is film and cantata. He could write a sarcastic march or a melody of enormous length. His style is not a straight line but a rotation of three faces: irony, lyricism and epic.

What to hear

Start with rhythm: mechanical repetition, sudden accents and key shifts make the music seem to dance like a machine. Then follow melody, whose lyrical themes often leap across a wide register. In the piano sonatas, notice percussive chords and toccata-like runs. Finally listen to the scoring, where woodwind and brass often behave like cartoon characters, expressive from their first note.

Key works

Classical Symphony; Romeo and Juliet; Peter and the Wolf; Alexander Nevsky; Piano Sonatas Nos. 6, 7 and 8; War and Peace.

Influence and misreadings

He gave ballet, film and children's music a modernist edge and made irony a legitimate part of serious music. Shostakovich, film composers and musical theatre all learned from his rhythm and orchestration. He proved that modern music need not keep listeners at a distance; it can hit like a stage gesture.
