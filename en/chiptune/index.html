Chiptune

Music made with the sound chips of old game consoles, where limits became a timbre.

1 min readGenres and LineageUpdated 2026-10-28

Chiptune uses the sound chips of old game machines, where limits became a timbre.

Origins and era

From the late 1970s, arcade and home consoles generated sound on dedicated chips. The NES had two pulse channels, a triangle, noise and a sample; the Game Boy and Commodore 64 had fixed counts. Composers fit melody, bass and drums into that budget. Sampled audio displaced the chips in the 1990s, chiptune moved underground, and since the 2000s festivals and plug-ins revived it.

What you hear

Pulse waves carry the melody, the triangle the bass, and noise the drums and effects. Chords are often fast arpeggios in which three or four notes take turns. There is no reverb or sample, the image is hard and the dynamic range narrow. Tracks are short loops, varied by fills and sections. A high melody over a leaping bass is typical.

Key figures

Koji Kondo wrote chip themes for Super Mario and Zelda, and Hirokazu Tanaka set the atmosphere of Metroid and Mother. Nobuo Uematsu used chip sound in early Final Fantasy, Anamanaguchi brought chiptune into rock, and Disasterpeace scored Fez with chips.

Lineage

8-bit console sound, arcade effects and the demoscene feed in; modern chiptune, indie game scores and hyperpop come out.

Common misunderstandings

Chiptune is not all game music, since games have long used orchestral and sampled scores. It is not low-quality music, as channel limits demand precise allocation. It is not only nostalgia, because new work is still made on modern hardware and plug-ins.
