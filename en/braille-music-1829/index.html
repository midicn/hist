Braille Music Notation

In 1829 Louis Braille turned music into raised dots, so blind musicians could read scores unaided.

1 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-28

braillenotationaccessibilityParis

Related

RomanticOrganPiano

The year it happened

Paris, 1829. Louis Braille, twenty, published a method for writing words, music and plainchant in raised dots at the Royal Institute for Blind Youth.

The event itself

Blind from three, Braille learned raised letters at the institute, but a page held only a few lines. He packed each character into six dots in a two-by-three cell, giving sixty-four combinations. Music used the same cells. Dot position gave pitch; the number and arrangement of dots gave duration, accidentals and octave. Fingers sweep one line, then the next, and polyphony is written part by part or hand by hand.

What it left behind

Blind musicians could read without a reader. The Paris institute became a centre for braille music. National codes multiplied, an international agreement emerged, and the standard was revised in 1997.

Hear the turn

Bach's chorale prelude on Wer nur den lieben Gott lässt walten is clear in four parts; Walther's prelude on Lobe den Herrn uses a chorale tune as its frame; Come, Come, Ye Saints is a four-part hymn with even spacing. Braille music writes such textures part by part.
