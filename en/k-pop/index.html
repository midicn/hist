K-Pop

Group line-ups, choreography and trainee systems built Seoul's idol industry after 1992.

K-pop names the idol pop industry that took shape in South Korea in the 1990s, built on group line-ups, choreography and intensive production, starting in Seoul in 1992.

Era and place

In 1992 Seo Taiji and Boys performed I Know on a television talent show, putting rap, rock guitar and dance beats into Korean pop and marking the start. Lee Soo-man founded SM Entertainment in 1995, H.O.T. debuted in 1996, Sechs Kies followed in 1997, and BoA and TVXQ later expanded the market into Japan and the rest of Asia. Trainee systems, choreographers and visual directors formed an industrial process.

What you hear

Tempos usually run from 100 to 130 BPM, drums play a four-beat dance pulse, and a rap verse or breakdown often precedes the chorus. Arrangements are layered, with synthesizers, brass samples and guitar taking turns, and voices distributed by role, so that lead singer, sub-vocalist and rapper each have a place. Choruses are short and repetitive and usually carry one easy dance move. Lyrics are mostly Korean with English phrases, and the mix pushes voices far forward. Choruses often carry a wordless synth hook, bridges drop the tempo or turn to minor before the major chorus returns, and vocals are thickly stacked with breath left in. The first thirty seconds must supply a hook, a requirement tied directly to television and short-video circulation.

Key figures

Seo Taiji brought rock and rap into the mainstream, and Lee Soo-man built the trainee and group-production model. H.O.T. and Sechs Kies defined the first idol generation, BoA carried the market into Japan, and TVXQ with later groups scaled touring and fandom. Producers, choreographers and stylists matter as much as the singers.

Lines before and after

Korean popular song, American rap and dance pop, and the Japanese idol industry feed in; the global idol market and short-video circulation of the 2010s come out. It turned group, choreography and video into an exportable product and changed how pop musicians are trained across Asia.

Common misunderstandings

The common claim is that K-pop is a musical style, when it is closer to an industry and a production method containing dance, ballad, rap and rock. Another is that it targets only Korean listeners, though export was the goal from the late 1990s. Equating K-pop with all Korean popular music also misses ballad singers, independent bands and trot.
