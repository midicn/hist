Roy Agnew

A Sydney piano teacher who wrote Australian landscape into impressionist harmony

A life in brief

Roy Agnew (1891–1944) was born in Sydney and died there, having hardly left Australia. He earned his living as a pianist, teacher and examiner, composed in whatever time was left, and wrote almost nothing but short solo piano pieces. The titles are plain — Australian Forest Pieces, Trains, An Autumn Morning, Green Valley. Local teachers used his music while he lived; it vanished soon after his death, and only recent recordings have put his name back on programmes.

The city he worked in

In the early twentieth century Australia had no real composing profession: orchestras and conservatories were just starting, and composers paid their bills by teaching and examining. Agnew sits between late romanticism and French impressionism. His harmony has Debussy's parallel and whole-tone colour, and a Russian weight in the bass, but he packs it into very short pieces with landscape titles. National label plus international language was common in the English-speaking world, and it left him neither modern nor folk enough for either camp.

What to hear

Listen first for colour. The opening chord of An Autumn Morning does not resolve; it hangs like fog, and as the bass creeps the chord above changes tint. Then for mechanical repetition. Trains repeats one figure and adds speed and weight, making motion out of repetition alone. Next, landscape as composition. Australian Forest Pieces is a set of short scenes, each built from a held bass, high tremolo and thin octaves. Finally, the ending. His pieces often close unsettled, on a chord that answers nothing.

Key works

  • Australian Forest Pieces, Rural Sketches, Holiday Suite
  • 3 Preludes, 3 Poems, 3 Lyrics
  • Trains, Rabbit Hill, An Autumn Morning, Drifting Mists

What remains

His music circulated in a small circle while he lived and was hard to find afterwards. Composers like this remind us that the twentieth-century map is not made only of big names; in every city without a centre someone was writing with local scenery and imported technique. Recent recordings have made the piano pieces audible again, and given Australian impressionism a concrete example rather than a label.
