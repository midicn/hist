Electrical Recording, 1925

In 1925 electrical recording gave dynamics to the microphone and made intimate singing possible.

1 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-28

electrical-recordingmicrophonepopular-song1925

Related

ModernMicrophoneVoice

When, and where

New York and London, 1925. Columbia and Victor adopted the Western Electric system, ending acoustic recording through a horn.

First, the event

The old system placed players before a horn whose diaphragm cut the groove directly, cutting low and high frequencies and forcing the band close together. The new system turned sound into an electrical signal, amplified it and drove a cutter. Frequency response widened, volume came under a knob, and singers could work at different distances.

What was different after

The microphone reshaped arranging and singing. A singer no longer had to overpower a band and could use a speaking register at low volume; crooning followed. Dance bands added guitar, muted trumpet and strings, balanced through the microphone.

Hear the turn

Texas Tommy Swing is a 1910s dance number; hear the density of the tutti and the hard edge of the drums, written for dance-hall volume. Morton's The Crave is solo piano, its bass heavy and its layers carried by the fingers. Gershwin's Do it Again, from 1922, has a narrow range and short phrases, made for quiet singing at the microphone.
