Southern Rock

Twin-guitar blues, boogie rhythm and long improvisation came out of Florida and Georgia in the 1970s.

Related

ContemporaryElectric guitarHammond organBeat

Southern rock put blues improvisation, twin-guitar interplay and country rhythm into a rock band, forming in Florida and Georgia in the late 1960s.

Origins and era

The Allman Brothers Band formed in Jacksonville in 1969 and soon moved its base to Macon, Georgia, working with Capricorn Records. Lynyrd Skynyrd also came from Jacksonville, formed in 1964 and broke through after 1973. The Marshall Tucker Band in South Carolina and Charlie Daniels in Nashville widened the sound. Duane Allman died in 1971 and Skynyrd's plane crashed in 1977, and the two events closed the scene early.

What you hear

Tempos run from slow blues to a 120 BPM boogie, with shuffle or two-and-four drums and bass locked to the kick. Two electric guitars play different parts of one melody, or one keeps rhythm while the other takes a long solo built on the minor pentatonic and string bends. Hammond organ fills underneath, and country-style high harmony or gospel-style choral singing appears now and then. Tracks often pass five minutes, live versions longer, while bass and drums keep pushing through the solos instead of adding fills. The organist uses a rotating speaker for a swirling tone. Through long improvisations the drummer holds one steady landing point, and the bass repeats short figures without rewriting the harmony.

Key figures

Duane Allman's slide guitar and Gregg Allman's keyboards and voice set the template, and Dickey Betts wrote the more melodic lines. Skynyrd's Gary Rossington and Allen Collins, with singer Ronnie Van Zant, carried the three-guitar line-up and narrative lyrics to radio. Charlie Daniels joined fiddle to southern storytelling, and the Marshall Tucker Band opened another colour with flute and country material.

Lineage

Electric blues, country music and 1960s British blues rock feed in; the 1990s southern rock revival and country rock come out. It returned American regional accents to the role of rock lead singer and made the twin-guitar conversation a fixed part of live performance. Radio carried the sound nationwide in the 1970s and gave it an audience shared with country music. Later bands borrowed the twin guitars and the long jams but often dropped the blues layer.

Common misunderstandings

The common claim is that southern rock equals the Confederate flag and conservative politics. That association mostly formed in the 1970s and remains contested, while the music borrows heavily from Black blues. Another is that the style is only long solos, when three-minute radio singles were just as central to the scene.
