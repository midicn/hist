Trap

808 bass, half-time snares and dense hi-hats turn rap into low-end architecture.

Trap turns rap into low-end architecture from 808 bass, half-time snares and dense hi-hats.

Era and place

In early-2000s Atlanta, producers combined the 808 drum machine's bass, fast hi-hats and half-time snares into a new backing. T.I. and Gucci Mane first made it street records, and in the 2010s Future and Migos pushed it into global rap.

What you hear

Start with the low end, where the 808 bass note is stretched into something felt as much as heard. The snare lands on three, giving a half-time feel, while kicks stay sparse. Hi-hats roll in sixteenths and thirty-seconds, often with triplets. Melody comes from minor-key synths and bells in a cold space. Voices are layered with Auto-Tune, and the flow is short and repetitive.

Key figures

T.I. made trap an album concept, Gucci Mane defined the underground through volume, Future treated the voice as timbre, and Migos rewrote rhythm with triplet flows.

Lines before and after

Southern rap, the 808 machine and dance music's low-end aesthetic feed in; emo rap, drill and pop-rap hooks come out. Bass became structure rather than decoration.

Common misunderstandings

Trap is not defined by crime lyrics, since the sound can stand without them. Nor is it simply slow, as half-time comes from snare placement, not tempo. Treating it as an Atlanta local style misses how it rewrote bass and drums in global pop.
