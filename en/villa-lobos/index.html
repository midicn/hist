Heitor Villa-Lobos

He fused Bach with Brazilian folk rhythm into a homegrown modernism

A life in brief

Heitor Villa-Lobos (1887–1959) was born in Rio de Janeiro, where his father was an amateur musician. He learned a little cello as a boy but spent more time listening to street choro bands; as a young man he travelled the Brazilian interior and collected folk melodies. In 1923 he went to Paris, returned in 1930 to work in music education, and became an internationally known composer late in life.

The city he worked in

Villa-Lobos is the representative composer of Brazilian musical modernism. The Modern Art Week of 1922 pushed "Brazilianness" to the fore, and he answered it in music: neither copying European modern techniques nor moving folk songs whole into the hall, but letting Bachian counterpoint and Brazilian urban rhythm seep into each other. The nine Bachianas brasileiras and fourteen Chôros are the centre of that path.

What to hear

Listen first for the breath of the choro: this Rio street ensemble has a loose, improvisatory feel, its melody close to speech. Then the skeleton of counterpoint: subjects enter, imitate and chase, Bach's devices used to organise folk material. Then guitar writing: he knew the instrument's open strings and positions, and his chords can strike like percussion. Finally stacked rhythms: several patterns run at once, pushing the dance toward collective festivity.

Key works

  • Bachianas brasileiras Nos. 1, 2, 4, 5
  • Chôros Nos. 1, 5, 10
  • Rudepoema, Twelve Guitar Études

What remains

Villa-Lobos gave Brazilian music its own voice within modernism and left core repertoire for the guitar. He did not treat folk music as raw material but as a language in which fugues could be written.
