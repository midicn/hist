Medieval Music

From plainchant to polyphony, the church, notation and the first named composers shaped Western music's beginnings.

2 min readPeriodsUpdated 2026-10-28

The Middle Ages is called a dark age, yet the thousand years between Rome's fall and the Renaissance produced Western notation, modal theory, polyphony and the first named composers. The church was the main employer; music was first a technology of prayer.

The world behind the music

After Rome collapsed, monasteries and cathedrals preserved books and music. Charlemagne's late-eighth-century reforms spread Roman chant; towns, universities and Gothic cathedrals grew from the twelfth century, giving longer music a place. The Black Death and the Hundred Years' War weakened church authority, and musical life moved toward courts and lay listeners.

What the music does

Chant is monophonic and has no fixed beat: the melody follows Latin stress, and mode is heard through the final and the range. From the ninth century singers added a parallel voice below the chant, and organum was born. At Notre Dame the upper voice grew florid, setting a sustained tenor against fast figuration. Mensural notation let voices align in measured time, so polyphony could be composed rather than improvised. The Ars Nova opened rhythm and chromaticism further.

Genres and forms

The Mass sets Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus and Agnus Dei as a cycle. The motet lays different texts over a chant tenor, devotional work and technical display. Secular song includes the troubadour chanson, ballade, rondeau and virelai, where love, politics and satire enter music. Laude brought congregations into devotion.

Instruments and forces

Voices are central. The church avoided instruments as lacking meaning; the organ was the exception, supporting chant and enlarging the sound. Portative organs, vielle, harp, lute, recorder and shawm appear in courts, doubling voices or playing dances.

Composers

  • Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179): abbess of Rupertsberg; her monophonic songs range widely and leap boldly, like dictated visions.
  • Léonin (late 12th century): he shaped organum at Notre Dame into sections with clear rises and falls.
  • Pérotin (late 12th to early 13th century): expanded organum to three and four voices, with repeating rhythmic patterns driving the sound.
  • Guillaume de Machaut (1300–1377): poet and composer; his Messe de Nostre Dame makes the ordinary a unified cycle, and his secular polyphony is equally intricate.

What to listen for

Begin with chant: do not count beats, follow the pause at each phrase's end. Then hear organum: the lowest voice stretches the chant while the upper voice moves above it, the first lesson in layered time. Finally hear Machaut's Mass: the same words sound in several voices, and each syllable should stay clear. Many library versions use synthetic timbres, so listen for voice-leading rather than historical instruments.

China, at the same time

In the same millennium China moved from the Southern and Northern Dynasties to the early Ming. Tang court yanyue and faqu were large-scale, Song ci joined melody to irregular verse, guqin and gongche notation matured, and Buddhist and Daoist ritual used music widely. Both worlds began with oral tradition and ritual, but Chinese notation served a single line.
