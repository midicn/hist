The Gospel Song

In 1875 revival songbooks turned solo verse and chorus into a publishing category

2 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-30

music historyhymnAmerican musicpublishing

Related

RomanticHarmoniumStrophic FormOrgan

In 1875 the songbooks of Moody and Sankey turned the gospel song into a publishing category. Solo verse, repeated chorus and portable organ became the standard equipment of mass meetings.

The year it happened

Chicago, London and Boston between 1873 and 1875. After a British campaign, the evangelist Dwight L. Moody and the singer Ira Sankey returned to the United States and issued Gospel Hymns and Sacred Songs, enlarged again and again. Sankey sang solo while Moody preached, and the books were sold at meetings and Sunday schools.

The sequence of events

The gospel song was built for a meeting: a memorable tune, a chorus everyone could repeat, words that spoke directly of salvation and invitation, and often a portable organ for accompaniment. Sankey's The Ninety and Nine is said to have been written on the spot after a sermon in Scotland; the story circulated widely, but the song's structure explains more. A solo verse narrates, the chorus turns to the audience, and the harmony uses popular idioms rather than the church chorale style. The books were cheap and printed in quantity, distributed through the revival network.

What was different after

An industry of mass song appeared, with songbooks, solo singers, touring campaigns and instrument sales reinforcing each other. Gospel song harmony and chorus form entered later popular and country music. The term itself was later taken up by Black churches and grew into a completely different gospel tradition. The two lines share a name but not an origin or a sound.

Listen for the turn

Begin with Sankey's The Ninety and Nine, the contrast between verse and chorus. Then He Is Coming, where chromatic harmony makes the waiting tense. Finally Bliss's Let the Lower Lights Be Burning, the same verse-and-chorus shape with a tune closer to popular narrative song. The catalogue offers organ and vocal arrangements; hear where the chorus lifts the room.
