Jean-Baptiste Lully

The founder of French opera, who turned court ballet into sung tragedy

1 min read0 characters条目ComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

Life

Jean-Baptiste Lully (1632–1687) was born in Florence and came to the French court at fourteen, starting as a servant and dancer before becoming Louis XIV's most trusted musician. He ran the royal music and secured the privilege of the Royal Academy of Music, writing a new kind of opera in French.

His place

Lully stood at the centre of French classical court culture. With Molière he created the comédie-ballet, putting drama, dance and song on one stage. After gaining the opera privilege in 1672 he founded the tragédie en musique, moving the plot with recitative and closing scenes with chorus and dance. His music follows French accent and syntax rather than Italian coloratura.

What to hear

Listen first for French recitative: it does not settle into Italian melody but follows the rise and fall of the spoken line, like speech held up by music. Then hear the dances: minuet, chaconne and gavotte keep returning, over a firm and clear bass. Finally the overture: the slow-fast-slow French overture, whose dotted rhythm announces court grandeur the moment it begins.

Key works

  • Tragédies en musique: Armide, Atys, Alceste
  • Comédies-ballets: Le Bourgeois gentilhomme, L'Amour médecin
  • Sacred vocal: Salve Regina, Te Deum

Influence and misreadings

Lully fixed the grammar of French opera: recitative, chorus, dance and stage machinery as one whole. His overtures were borrowed by Bach and Handel, and his orchestral discipline became a French tradition. He made opera in France a national language rather than an Italian imitation.
