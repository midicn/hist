Baroque Music

Basso continuo, major-minor tonality and dramatic expression gave birth to opera, the concerto and the fugue.

2 min readPeriodsUpdated 2026-10-28

The Baroque moved music from equal voices to harmony supported by a bass. A sustained bass, major and minor keys and a dramatic manner of expression let opera, concerto and fugue grow from one grammar.

The world behind the music

Seventeenth-century Europe was shaped by absolute monarchy, colonial trade and the scientific revolution. Louis XIV turned Versailles into a power stage, with opera and ballet as court ceremony; Venice opened a ticket-selling opera house in 1637, and music moved toward the public. By the Enlightenment, musicians still worked for courts and churches, but the idea of a work and its composer had become clearer.

What the music does

Basso continuo is the skeleton: a keyboard or lute fills in chords from figures, a cello or bassoon holds the bass, upper voices move freely. Major-minor tonality replaces church modes, and the motion from dominant to tonic becomes the strongest punctuation. Composers aim for clear feelings, using repeated figures, ornaments and dynamic contrast. The concerto alternates tutti and solo, while the fugue lets the subject run through the voices.

Genres and forms

Opera takes shape in 1600, joining drama, poetry, scenery and music into one art; oratorio and cantata place sacred narrative in arias, recitatives and choruses. Instrumental music includes the trio sonata, concerto grosso, solo concerto, suite, passacaglia and fugue. Allemande, courante, sarabande and gigue become a dance language.

Instruments and forces

Strings become the core of the orchestra, with the violin family replacing viols; harpsichord, organ and lute supply the continuo. Woodwinds are mainly recorder, Baroque flute, oboe and bassoon, while natural trumpet and horn can play only the harmonic series, which limits their writing. Timpani join court bands; timbres are bright with short resonance.

Composers

  • Monteverdi (1567–1643): moved from madrigal to opera; L'Orfeo joins monody to instrumental colour.
  • Corelli (1653–1713): established the writing of the trio sonata and concerto grosso.
  • Vivaldi (1678–1741): a driving force behind the solo concerto; The Four Seasons paints nature in figures.
  • Bach (1685–1750): fugue, chorale prelude and concerto became a systematic language in his hands.
  • Handel (1685–1759): a master of opera, oratorio and court instrumental music; Messiah cast a long shadow.

What to listen for

Follow the bass: continuo motion decides where the music is going. Then hear the alternation of tutti and solo; the concerto's returns arrive in the same music and give stable direction. Finally hear a fugue: the subject appears alone, then each entry changes voice or key. Some library versions are keyboard arrangements, so listen to voice-leading before timbre.

China, at the same time

In the late Ming and early Qing, Chinese opera flourished. Kunqu was polished in literati circles, while Yiyang and bangzi styles spread locally; pipa, qin and court ritual music each continued their own line. Europe was systematising harmony and bass, China was systematising vocal style and metre.
