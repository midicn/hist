Country Blues

A single acoustic guitar accompanies its own singer, with alternating bass and melody on the top strings

2 min readGenres and LineageUpdated 2026-10-30

bluescountryguitarAmerican South

Related

Country blues is the solo blues of the rural American South in the 1920s and 1930s. One acoustic guitar accompanies its own singer, the bass alternates between two strings, the melody runs on the top strings, and phrases breathe more than in city blues.

Origins and era

It spread across the South in several regional branches. The Piedmont on the East Coast is known for fingerpicking and alternating bass, Texas for single-note guitar and high falsetto, and the Mississippi Delta for slide. The "race records" of the 1920s put these players on disc: Blind Lemon Jefferson began recording for Paramount in 1926, Blind Blake left a large body of fingerstyle pieces between 1926 and 1932, and Mississippi John Hurt recorded Avalon Blues for Okeh in 1928.

What you hear

The guitar carries bass, chords and melody at once, the thumb keeping a steady alternation on the low strings while index and middle fingers pluck the tune above. Tempos are usually unhurried, phrases can pause or repeat a whole verse, and lyrics often use the three-line blues stanza but also longer ballad-like stretches. Recordings are solo, without drums or bass, with audible room tone and surface noise.

Key figures

Blind Lemon Jefferson brought Texas single-note singing to records, Blind Blake's fingerpicking was almost pianistic, and Mississippi John Hurt was known for a gentle voice and steady alternating bass. Furry Lewis, Reverend Gary Davis and Elizabeth Cotten carried the tradition into the folk revival.

Lineage

Southern ballads, dance tunes and spirituals feed in, and the folk revival, skiffle, the British blues boom and rock come out. It shares the country blues category with the Delta style, differing in the balance of slide and fingerpicking.

Common misunderstandings

Country blues is not simply blues without electricity; it is a way of arranging a guitar and a voice. Nor is it all Delta-style slide and moaning, since the Piedmont and Texas fingerpicking traditions matter just as much. Reducing it to blind street singers leaves out the many players who worked in churches and homes.
