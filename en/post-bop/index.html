Post-Bop

Mid-1960s quintets loosen harmony, metre and texture at once without giving up the composed frame

2 min readGenres and LineageUpdated 2026-10-30

Post-bop names a set of jazz approaches that appeared in the mid-1960s. The frame of theme, solos and ensemble stays, while harmony, metre and the drums' interaction loosen at once, so the music still sounds composed.

Where it came from

Between 1963 and 1968 Blue Note and Columbia recorded this music in New York. Miles Davis's quintet settled in 1964, with Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter and Tony Williams treating rehearsal as composition. Shorter's Footprints used triple metre and modal scales, Hancock's Maiden Voyage a pedal bass, and Davis's ESP pulled the beat apart. The label came later from critics; the players spoke of playing something new.

What the ear catches

Harmony stops resolving on schedule, using pedals, modal scales and ambiguous major-minor shading. Triple and quintuple metres alternate with irregular sections, and the drums answer the soloist with colour and accent rather than counting time. The bass sometimes holds a long note while piano or vibraphone leaves space with sparse chords. It may also walk, then repeat one note for a whole section, while the piano comps with two- or three-note voicings. Themes are short and symmetrical, yet solos can travel far before returning.

Names you cannot avoid

Davis's second quintet is the centre, Shorter's writing supplied much of its material, Hancock's piano and composing pushed modal harmony toward pop, and Williams's drums cut time into fragments. Joe Henderson, Andrew Hill and Bobby Hutcherson wrote equally open pieces from other directions.

What it influenced

Hard bop, modal jazz and free jazz all feed in as partial techniques inside single pieces. Fusion, M-Base and modern jazz composition come out, and the repertoire shaped countless contemporary quintets. It also gave later bands a way to sound modern without abandoning written themes.

What is often misread

Post-bop is not everything after bebop, or free jazz and fusion would be swallowed by it. It means the mid-1960s bands that still wrote tunes and still improvised while loosening harmony and metre. Nor is it free jazz, since themes and sections usually remain, only with wider borders.
