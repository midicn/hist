Marc-Antoine Charpentier

The Frenchman who brought Roman technique home and wrote the Te Deum prelude

Related

BaroqueMassOratorio

Who he was

Marc-Antoine Charpentier (1643–1704) was a French composer of the Baroque. As a young man he went to Rome and studied with Carissimi, bringing Italian recitative, choral writing and instrumental practice back to Paris. After his return he served the household of the Duchesse de Guise for many years, then worked at the Jesuit church of Saint-Louis, and from 1698 was master of music at the Sainte-Chapelle. He wrote masses, motets, oratorios, operas and a great deal of occasional sacred music; the scholar H. Wiley Hitchcock later catalogued it with H numbers.

His place

He stands between France and Italy. Lully fixed French court opera around recitative, while Charpentier used Italian melody and French dance rhythm side by side in sacred music. His oratorios are among the earliest in France, and his religious works were written for particular monasteries and feast days, small in scale but enormous in number. The opera Médée of 1693 shows that he could speak Lully's language; he simply never entered the inner circle of the court.

What to hear

Listen first for what a prelude does. The Te Deum H. 146 opens with a fanfare figure, a short phrase repeated, that tells the listener a ceremony is starting; this music later became the theme of the Eurovision Song Contest. Then listen for dance rhythm. The Marche en rondeau sets the pace of a march plainly, the bass like footsteps and the treble like an answer. Finally listen for borrowed melody. The Messe de minuit puts several French Christmas carols into the sections of the mass, so follow whether the familiar tune lands in the top voice or inside the texture.

Key works

  • Te Deum H. 146, with its prelude and Marche en rondeau
  • Messe de minuit pour Noël, H. 9
  • Opera Médée (1693) and the sacred drama David et Jonathas (1688)
  • Les Arts florissants, H. 487

Influence

He brought Italian practice into French sacred music in a systematic way, and French motets and oratorios afterwards had a wider vocabulary. In the late twentieth century this repertory was edited and recorded, and Charpentier changed from a contemporary of Lully into a subject in his own right; the wide circulation of that prelude also sent listeners back to his masses and oratorios.
