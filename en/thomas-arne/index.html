Thomas Arne

The London theatre composer who wrote Rule, Britannia! and the Shakespeare songs

2 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-30

composerclassicalenglandopera

Related

ClassicalMasqueOrgan

Training and teachers

Thomas Arne (1710–1778) was the leading English theatre composer of the eighteenth century. He was the son of a London upholsterer, educated at Eton and destined for the law, but taught himself music and entered the theatre as a violinist. He wrote music for Comus in 1738 and for the masque Alfred in 1740, whose finale, Rule, Britannia!, became almost a second national anthem. He wrote songs for the Vauxhall Gardens for years, and his Artaxerxes of 1762, sung in Italian, was a rare serious opera on the London stage.

His place

He belongs to the London that followed Handel. Handel had brought Italian opera seria to England and also brought it into crisis; Arne took a different route, writing mainly English stage works, masques, incidental music, songs and glees for audiences in theatres and pleasure gardens. His melodies are direct, his harmony clear and his phrases short, easier to sing along with than Handel's, which is exactly the character the commercial London theatre needed.

Where to start

Listen first for how a number opens. Rule, Britannia! begins with a band introduction that fixes key and pace, and when the voice enters the texture and volume suddenly thin out, which is where the drama lies. Then listen for the division between recitative and aria. In Artaxerxes the recitative pushes the plot and the aria stops to unfold a feeling, and the rhythmic difference between them is large. Finally listen for the weight of the words. In his Shakespeare settings the accents follow the text, and the accompaniment changes harmony at the key words.

Works and craft

  • Masque Alfred (1740), including Rule, Britannia!
  • Opera Artaxerxes (1762)
  • Incidental music for Comus (1738) and the song Blow, Blow, Thou Winter Wind
  • Theatre music for The Miller of Mansfield and songs for Vauxhall Gardens

Influence and misreadings

He turned English stage music into a sustainable professional field, and later British theatre music and songwriting follow that line. Rule, Britannia! entered national ceremony and the choral tradition, and his Shakespeare songs still appear in song recitals. Any account of eighteenth-century English music that stops at Handel is incomplete.
