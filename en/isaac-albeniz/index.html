Isaac Albéniz

The travelling prodigy who put Iberia and Andalusia into the piano

Related

RomanticPianoCharacter Piece

Who he was

Isaac Albéniz (1860–1909) was born in Camprodon, Girona, and died at Cambo-les-Bains in France. A child prodigy, he played in public early and toured Spain and the Americas with his family as a boy, and is said to have earned his living at the keyboard in Cuba and the United States. In 1876 he entered the Brussels Conservatory to study with Brassin; in the 1880s he worked in Madrid and Paris, and in the 1890s he settled in Paris among Fauré, Debussy and Dukas. Kidney disease killed him in France in 1909.

His place

His life spans two identities. The early one is the virtuoso who wrote salon pieces and Spanish rhapsodies and made his market with works such as the Suite española; the late one, encouraged by Pedrell, turned to folk material and produced the twelve pieces of Iberia in four books, with harmony and texture close to French Impressionism. He held no fixed post and did not conduct, and his output is almost entirely for the piano. Iberia, written between 1905 and 1909, is his final account of Spanish landscape and dance.

What to hear

Listen first for the guitar in the bass. Asturias from the Suite española Op.47 uses a held bass against fast figures to imitate plucked strings; hear the bass return like an open string. Then the frame of the dance. The tango and the Malagueña in España Op.165 have plain rhythms and clear accents, a good place to fix the beat. Then late harmony. In Iberia the same dance is filled with semitones and remote modulations; the tune survives while the key is left hanging. Finally the writing of colour. La Vega uses tremolos and fast scales for light and heat, and the piano approaches the layering of an orchestra.

Key works

  • Piano: Iberia, Suite española Op.47, España Op.165, La Vega, Navarra
  • Opera: Pepita Jiménez
  • Arrangements: the orchestral version of Iberia completed by Arbós

Influence

He carried Spanish piano music from the salon into the concert programme. The harmony and texture of Iberia gave Spanish and French composers a shared way of hearing Spain, and the Spanish works of Debussy and Ravel draw on the same material. The pieces are so hard that small hands can scarcely play them whole, so they were often arranged for guitar and found a second life in the guitar repertoire. His opera and songs are rarely staged today, and his name rests on the piano music.
