Alexander Borodin

A chemistry professor who wrote the steppes and the Polovtsian Dances

1 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

A life in brief

Alexander Borodin (1833–1887) was born in St Petersburg, the illegitimate son of a Georgian prince, and took his surname from one of his father's household serfs. His main profession was chemistry: he studied medicine, earned a doctorate and taught chemistry at the Medico-Surgical Academy, composing only in his spare time. He belonged to Balakirev's circle, later called the Mighty Handful. Prince Igor remained unfinished after eighteen years and was completed by Rimsky-Korsakov and Glazunov.

The city he worked in

He is one of the central figures of the Russian national school. Unlike a professional composer, his music carries a laboratory patience and an amateur's freedom: nothing is rushed, yet the phrases are often extraordinarily beautiful. His Second Symphony is nicknamed the Bogatyr, and In the Steppes of Central Asia draws a landscape in sound.

What to hear

Listen first for oriental melody: the women's tune in the Polovtsian Dances is broad and decorated, the steppe as Russia imagined it. Then static harmony: In the Steppes hardly develops themes at all, creating distance through pedal notes and shifting colour. Next, irregular accents: the dances contain passages that refuse to stay in four. Finally, chamber lyricism: the slow movement of the Second Quartet was later arranged for solo instruments, so long is its line.

Key works

  • Opera: Prince Igor, including the Polovtsian Dances
  • Orchestral: In the Steppes of Central Asia, Symphony No. 2
  • Chamber: String Quartet No. 2
  • Piano: Scherzo, Petite Suite

What remains

His harmony and scoring shaped Rimsky-Korsakov and the French school, and the Polovtsian Dances remain among the most memorable Russian tunes in the concert hall. He proved that an amateur's status need not stand in the way of professional music.
