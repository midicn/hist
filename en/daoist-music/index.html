Daoist Music

Daoist music joins chant and instruments to carry the jiao rites, with Quanzhen and Zhengyi lines each keeping their own tradition.

Time and place

Daoist music travelled with the jiao rites. It had grown substantial by the Northern and Southern Dynasties, entered Tang and Song courts, and settled into local temples in the Ming and Qing. Quanzhen uses the shared shifang yun, kept at the White Cloud Temple and Mount Wudang; Zhengyi uses local melodies, with Mount Longhu, Mount Mao and Suzhou's Xuanmiao Temple each having their own. It serves recitation, blessing and funerals.

Musical features

The music divides into yun and qu. Yun is chant, including Chengying Yun, Buxu Yun and Xiang Ji, paced slowly, answered by the congregation; qu is instrumental, played on sheng, dizi, guan and yunluo, with ritual percussion. Chants are mostly pentatonic, with long phrase ends held over a steady pulse. Zhengyi melodies lean on local speech and opera; Quanzhen is more uniform.

Repertoire

Chengying Yun, Buxu Yun, Sanbao Xiang and Xiang Ji belong to the rites; qupai such as Xiao Kaimen and Wannian Huan accompany the welcoming and sending off of deities. Daoqing later moved into the wider world as a storytelling genre.

What to listen for

Start with the steady points struck by the ritual instruments, then hear how chant long notes sit on that pulse. Sheng and flute fill the gaps and the yunluo adds colour. The same chant differs sharply between Quanzhen and Zhengyi singers.

Common misunderstandings

Daoist music is often reduced to plain recitation or confused with Buddhist chant. It has a full system of melody and instruments, and it differs by lineage and region. Nor is it a stage performance; the sound serves the order of the rite.
