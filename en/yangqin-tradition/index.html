The Yangqin Tradition

Struck with bamboo hammers, the yangqin came by sea to Guangdong in the late Ming and became the frame of Cantonese music and ballad singing

Yangqin (hammered dulcimer)

When and where

The yangqin reached Guangdong by sea in the late Ming and early Qing, first written yangqin, foreign zither, later renamed. It settled in the towns of Guangdong and Fujian, then followed trade routes into Jiangnan, Sichuan and the northeast. Cantonese and Shanghai silk-and-bamboo groups and the Sichuan ballad houses of Chengdu and Chongqing all placed it in the middle.

How it came about

Struck with two bamboo hammers, the yangqin lets one player supply melody and accompaniment at once, so it quickly became the harmonic and rhythmic anchor of ensembles. In Cantonese music Yan Laolie arranged tunes such as Thunder in Dry Weather and Lianhuan Kou for it, turning folk melodies into brilliant instrumental pieces; Jiangnan sizhu used it for harmony; in Sichuan it became the lead of the qinshu ballad. After the 1950s it entered conservatories with a larger body and chromatic layout.

The sound's frame

The instrument is a trapezoid wooden box with two or more rows of bridges, steel strings laid out by pitch, struck by bamboo hammers. The range usually exceeds three octaves, tuned in equal temperament and freely modulating, able to sound melody, double stops and arpeggios together. Techniques include single notes, double notes, tremolo, filler notes, staccato, sliding and trembling hammers; the tremolo turns one note into a line, the closest it comes to a bowed instrument.

What to listen for

Hear the hard attack of the hammer, then how tremolo spins a line. Cantonese playing is dense and ornamented; Sichuan playing follows the singer and leaves more space. The library's Hudie Feilai You Feiqu from Suzhou, the Jiangsu song praising the yangqin and the Chongqing tune are a Jiangnan melody, a song about the instrument and a Sichuan tune, not yangqin recordings.

What is often mistaken

The yangqin is often assumed to be Chinese in origin, or confused with the xylophone. It came from Persia and the Arab world and appeared in Guangdong only in the late Ming; a xylophone strikes wooden bars, a yangqin strikes steel strings, with a different resonance and range. Another mistake is to treat it as an accompanist only: in Cantonese music and in solos it is the main voice.
