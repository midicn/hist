Introduction to Xiqu

Xiqu is a total theatre of song, speech, dance and acrobatics, shaped by qupai and banqiang structures.

Time and place

Xiqu took shape in the Song and Yuan. Southern drama arose in Wenzhou; zaju flourished in the capital. Ming and Qing chuanqi was carried mainly by Kunqu, and in the Qing the hua tradition rose, with Peking Opera forming in the nineteenth century. It moved between temple stages, halls, theatres and courts.

Vocal style and musical features

Xiqu fuses song, speech, movement and acrobatics; roles divide into sheng, dan, jing and chou; performance is built on convention and suggestion. Singing follows either qupai form, which links fixed tunes with elaborate melisma, or banqiang form, which varies ban patterns to carry the plot in flexible couplets. The lead instrument follows the vocal family, with dizi for Kunqu, banhu for bangzi, jinghu for pihuang. Gongs and drums keep time and externalise feeling.

Representative plays

Guan Hanqing's The Injustice to Dou E, Wang Shifu's Romance of the Western Chamber, Tang Xianzu's The Peony Pavilion, Hong Sheng's The Palace of Eternal Life, Kong Shangren's The Peach Blossom Fan, plus Peking Opera's Farewell My Concubine and Yue Opera's The Butterfly Lovers.

What to listen for

Begin with Beijing's Flower Drum and the Shanghai-collected Fengyang Flower Drum, which keep the call-and-response of flower-drum plays; then Henan's Guzi San Qiang for the syntax of linked qupai. In the theatre, separate song from speech first, then hear how gongs and drums bind gesture to melody.

Common misunderstandings

Xiqu is often treated as the Chinese word for opera, or assumed to be all singing. Speech, gesture, combat and percussion matter equally; nor is it a frozen antique, since every era has changed tunes, plays and role types.
