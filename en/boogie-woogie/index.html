Boogie-Woogie

A left hand repeating eighth-note figures without a break while the right hand stacks short riffs, turning the blues into dance music

2 min readGenres and LineageUpdated 2026-10-30

bluespianoostinatodance

Related

Boogie-woogie is the most driven kind of piano blues. The left hand hammers the bass into an unbroken line of eighth notes while the right hand lays short phrases on top, leaving little room for sentiment.

Origins and era

It formed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in the logging camps, levee camps and railroad works of the American South, especially where east Texas, Louisiana and Arkansas meet. In December 1928 Pinetop Smith recorded Pinetop's Boogie Woogie in Chicago, and the name spread with the record. In December 1938 John Hammond's From Spirituals to Swing concert at Carnegie Hall put Meade Lux Lewis, Albert Ammons and Pete Johnson before a New York audience, and swing bands borrowed the bass figure in the 1940s.

What you hear

The left hand usually plays eight eighth notes to the bar, leaping in octaves or fifths, with a figure that settles after one or two bars and then barely changes. The right hand repeats short motives, often with blue notes and slides, clipping phrase ends with triplets or syncopation. Tempos are medium to fast, accents fall off the beat, and the effect is someone pushing a cart that will not stop. The form is usually twelve-bar blues, sometimes a popular song's harmony.

Key figures

Pinetop Smith gave the style its name and earliest model, while Jimmy Yancey played slowly and deeply, keeping the blues breath in it. Meade Lux Lewis, Albert Ammons and Pete Johnson became the New York trio after 1938, with heavier octaves and faster tempos. Cow Cow Davenport and Montana Taylor left rougher early portraits.

Lineage

It grew from Southern piano blues and barrelhouse playing, related to ragtime's left hand but built on a repeating figure rather than written strains. It feeds into the swing era, jump blues and early rock and roll, and shaped the left hand of later piano rock. It shares leaps with stride piano, but stride stays closer to popular-song harmony and melody.

Common misunderstandings

Boogie-woogie is not the "boogie" of 1970s disco, and not every blues pianist plays it. Slow blues piano without the unbroken eighth-note figure is closer to barrelhouse. Nor is it only American, since European pianists copied records in the late 1930s and local traditions grew elsewhere.
