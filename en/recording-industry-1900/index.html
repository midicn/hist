The Record Industry, 1900

The record industry turned sound into goods, creating stars, royalties and mass listening.

1 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-28

music historyrecordingrecord industrytechnology

Related

In 1900 the record became a business. Pressed discs, catalogues and celebrity recordings turned listening into household consumption.

The year it happened

London and New York, 1900. Berliner's Gramophone Company operated from London; Victor was founded in Camden in 1901, while Columbia and the Gramophone Company expanded. In 1902 Caruso recorded ten arias in Milan, and the record had its first global star.

First, the event

Discs displaced wax cylinders because they could be pressed, sleeved, catalogued and wholesaled. Factories turned out thousands of copies, and agents mailed catalogues to small towns. Recording was still acoustic: players faced a horn, the frequency range was narrow, and one side lasted minutes. Victor's Red Seal series made Caruso respectable home entertainment. Sousa, Joplin and Tin Pan Alley filled the catalogues.

Effects and aftermath

Music could repeat without a performer, and listening became consumption. Labels controlled recording, pressing and distribution, and stardom, royalties and charts followed. Repertoire bent to the three-minute side, and recording preserved performing habits for later researchers.

Listen for the turn

The library holds no original discs, only MIDI realisations. Begin with Caruso's Mattinata, then the Pagliacci aria, then Maple Leaf Rag and a Sousa march. Hear the short phrases and brass through the narrow band of a 78 rpm record.
