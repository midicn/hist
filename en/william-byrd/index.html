William Byrd

England's contrapuntal bridge between Latin motets, English anthems and virginal music

2 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-30

composerrenaissanceenglandpolyphony

Training and teachers

William Byrd (c. 1540–1623) was the leading English composer of the Elizabethan age. He became organist of Lincoln Cathedral in 1563 and moved to the Chapel Royal in London in 1572, working alongside the older Thomas Tallis. In 1575 the two were granted a patent for music printing in England and issued a joint volume of Latin motets. Byrd remained a Catholic all his life and kept writing Latin masses and liturgical music in a Protestant country, retiring late in life to Stondon Massey in Essex. He also left a large body of English anthems and virginal music.

His place

He spans two worlds. On one side stands the narrowing space for Catholic liturgy after the English Reformation; on the other, the London court and the new printing market. He wrote masses for three, four and five voices, modest in scale but produced under difficult conditions. For the keyboard he left pavans, galliards and variations, preserved in My Ladye Nevells Booke and the Fitzwilliam Virginal Book, among the first mature works of the English keyboard school.

Where to start

Listen first for what the number of voices means. In the three-part mass every line has to carry the harmony; there is no spare voice to hide in, so a single lapse is audible. Then listen for the difference between Latin and English. Latin motets run in long phrases; English anthems break at the words, often opening with a solid chord before imitation begins. Finally listen for how variation works. Each section usually changes one parameter only, rhythm, ornament or the number of voices, so you can hear where the change starts.

Works and craft

  • Masses for three, four and five voices
  • Motets, Miserere mei Deus and Sing Joyfully
  • Keyboard, The Woods So Wild and The Earl of Oxford's March
  • Songs, Psalmes, Sonets and Songs (1588)

Influence and misreadings

Byrd is the technical source of English polyphony. Gibbons, Tomkins and later Purcell inherited the same craft, and twentieth-century composers who edited his keyboard music and masses treated him as the country's first summit. His Latin music is also a reminder that Reformation England did not speak with one voice.
