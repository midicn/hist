Gioachino Rossini

The king of bel canto, who stopped writing operas at thirty-seven after William Tell

1 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

composerromanticitalyopera

Related

RomanticOperaAriaOverture

Training and teachers

Gioachino Rossini (1792–1868) was born in Pesaro to two working musicians. He wrote his first opera at eighteen and produced a new one almost every year for two decades. After William Tell opened in Paris in 1829 he stopped writing operas at thirty-seven, spent his later years in Paris and Bologna composing sacred music and salon pieces, and died in 1868.

His place

Rossini is the summation of Italian bel canto opera. He put melody back at the centre of the stage, joining vocal technique to comic timing, while in Paris he met the scale of grand opera and wrote William Tell in that French manner. Verdi and the whole bel canto tradition start from his grammar.

Where to start

Listen first for the vocal line: bel canto asks for an even tone across long phrases, and coloratura is not display but emotional measure. Then hear the crescendo: a phrase returns while scoring and volume thicken layer by layer, the famous Rossini crescendo that pins the audience's attention. Finally the ensemble close: several characters sing their own thoughts, then lock into one rhythm.

Works and craft

  • Il barbiere di Siviglia, La Cenerentola, L'italiana in Algeri
  • Guillaume Tell, Otello
  • Stabat Mater, the salon collection Péchés de vieillesse

Influence and misreadings

Rossini set the vocal standard for nineteenth-century Italian opera and helped make Paris a European opera capital. His overtures remain concert favourites, and later composers kept borrowing his crescendo. He wrote the weightiest tradition with the lightest comedy, then stopped at the peak.
