Debussy

The composer who made harmony into colour — though he disliked the "Impressionist" label

1 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

composerimpressionismfrancepiano

Related

ImpressionistScaleTimbrePiano

Life

Claude Debussy (1862–1918) was born near Paris and became one of France's most important modern composers. His output includes piano music, orchestral works, songs and the opera Pelléas et Mélisande. He is often called an "Impressionist composer", a label he himself disliked.

His place

Debussy stands after Romanticism and before modernism: he no longer uses harmony to "push" music forward but lets it appear all at once — blocks, parallel chords, whole-tone and pentatonic scales turn music from function into colour. His sensitivity to timbre, resonance and time directly shaped twentieth-century music.

What to hear

Listen first for the colour of harmony: do not ask where a chord is going; ask what it sounds like. Then hear layers of timbre: Debussy orchestrates like a painter, each woodwind, string and harp a colour. Finally hear the flow of time: his music often seems to stop, yet keeps changing inside.

Key works

  • Piano: Twelve Études, Préludes (two books), Images
  • Orchestral: La Mer, Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune, Nocturnes
  • Opera: Pelléas et Mélisande

Influence and misreadings

After Debussy, harmony is no longer only function but also colour; his timbral thinking shaped Ravel, Messiaen, film scoring and jazz. He turned music from "telling a story" into "presenting a moment".
