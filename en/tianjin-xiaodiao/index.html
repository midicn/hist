Tianjin Short Songs

Short songs of the Tianjin wharves, neatly narrative and hard-edged, closely related to the city's shidiao narrative singing.

Related

Ban and YanQupaiVoice

Time and place

Tianjin short songs belong to the city proper, to Yangliuqing and to the Jinghai country around it. They are sung in Tianjin speech, a form of Ji-Lu Mandarin with straight tones and hard endings. As a canal transport hub in the Ming and Qing, Tianjin drew boatmen, traders and entertainers from north and south, who brought their tunes and merged them with local speech into a style both urban and regular. Work calls belonged to the wharves, short songs to the lanes, and shidiao narrative singing to the teahouses.

What happened

In the late Qing and early Republic, teahouses and small theatres flourished, and short tunes moved from the lanes into paid performance, gradually separating into Tianjin shidiao. That genre was built on named modes such as Kaoshan, Yuanyang, New Yuanyang and Dashuzi, and acquired its present name in the 1950s as a form of narrative singing. The short songs stayed with ordinary life, at festivals, weddings and funerals. Collectors later recorded the two separately, songs as folk song and shidiao as narrative art.

Musical features

The songs are mostly four-phrase stanzas in duple time, square in shape and clear in narrative. Melody rests on a pentatonic frame, often zhi mode, moving by step rather than leap, with clean endings. The glottal stops and nasal finals of Tianjin speech shape the diction, which sounds blunter than Beijing song. Patter and enumeration are common for selling, listing dishes or naming months. Vocables are few and fixed in place.

What to listen for

Begin with The Clear River, a long narrative carried forward four phrases at a time, and notice how each stanza ends on a small suspense. Then take the Five Watches, one stanza per watch, where the sections mark the passage of time. The Twelve Months shows how months and flowers are ranged in order. End with Selling Dumplings, where the street vendor's manner is clearest and the patter runs out in a string.

Common misunderstandings

A common mistake is to call Tianjin shidiao a folk song. Shidiao is narrative art with fixed modes and performance conventions; short songs are folk song, with different settings and functions. Nor are these songs merely the comic verses heard in cross-talk, which form a separate line. And they are not all humorous: laments at weddings and funerals, and labourers' complaints, are equally part of the repertory.
