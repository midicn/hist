Robert Lowry

A Baptist minister who wrote the tunes for Shall We Gather at the River and Easter hymns

Training and teachers

Robert Lowry (1826–1899) was born in Philadelphia, educated at university, became a Baptist minister, serving churches in West Chester, Pennsylvania, Brooklyn and Plainfield, New Jersey. He wrote words as well as music. His best-known songs are Shall We Gather at the River (1864) and Low in the Grave He Lay (1874), and he supplied the tune for Fanny Crosby's All the Way My Savior Leads Me. He helped compile Sunday-school songbooks, dying in 1899.

His place

Lowry was a pastor and amateur composer, and that role fixed his occasions: worship, baptism, Easter, Sunday school. His tunes avoid complex harmony; they let a congregation grasp the melodic direction at first hearing. The words, his own or Crosby's, centre on images common in Baptist devotion: river, blood, resurrection, guidance. This is church music, judged by whether a room full of people can sing it, not by its effect in a concert hall.

Where to start

Hear first the flow of the melody. HANSON PLACE is the tune of Shall We Gather at the River: four phrases of similar length, gentle rise and fall, each ending with a slight descent, like water moving around stones. There are almost no leaps, so anyone can join. Then hear how contrast is built. Low in the Grave He Lay keeps the verse low and heavy, then the refrain lifts and quickens; resurrection is carried by pitch before explanation. Finally hear what ascent means. In All the Way My Savior Leads Me the melody climbs phrase by phrase and the refrain peaks before falling back, so guidance becomes a visible route. One track is a vocal score, two are organ arrangements.

Works and craft

  • Songs: Shall We Gather at the River, Low in the Grave He Lay, All the Way My Savior Leads Me
  • Others: Nothing but the Blood, Follow On, One More Day's Work for Jesus
  • Collections: Sunday-school and gospel hymnals

Influence and misreadings

Shall We Gather at the River is still sung at funerals and baptisms and has been arranged for instruments and popular singers; the Easter hymn remains a fixture in many churches. Lowry turned theological images into singable melody: river, blood and resurrection each get a matching figure. Later writers followed his order of work: first how a congregation sings, then where harmony goes.
