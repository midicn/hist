Techno

Detroit's machine funk, built on a straight four-on-the-floor pulse, cold chords and a futurist stance

Related

Techno is Detroit's machine funk from the 1980s, drawing an industrial city with a straight four-on-the-floor pulse, cold chords and synthetic timbres.

Origins and era

In the mid-1980s Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson, from Belleville outside Detroit, drew on radio electronic music, Kraftwerk and Parliament's funk. Atkins' Cybotron recorded Clear in 1983, handing Detroit its robot fantasy of electro funk; in 1987 May, recording as Rhythim Is Rhythim, released Strings of Life, whose string-like synth lifted the floor. A 1988 compilation put the name techno on this sound, Underground Resistance turned the label into a stance in 1989, refusing interviews and releasing everything themselves, and after 1991 Berlin's Tresor and its parades took over the floor.

What you hear

Tempos mostly run from 125 to 135 BPM. The kick lands on every beat without swing, hats and claps stay regular, and the snare usually falls on two and four. Chords are mostly minor, the timbres cold, short and filtered; synths play only a few notes and develop through repetition, small pitch shifts and filter sweeps. Bass tends to be a short synth note rather than a melody, and some tracks go whole sections with almost no harmonic change. Vocals are rare, and layers enter and leave to build the form, so the machine pulse keeps a trace of funk elasticity.

Key figures

Juan Atkins set the futurist outline with Clear and Model 500, Derrick May wrote feeling into the machine with Strings of Life, and Kevin Saunderson pushed techno toward the pop charts through Inner City. Jeff Mills rewrote what a DJ does with three decks and The Bells, Underground Resistance's Mad Mike Banks tied label, city politics and anonymity together, and in the 1990s Carl Craig and Robert Hood pulled techno toward thicker sampling and thinner minimalism.

Lineage

Kraftwerk, electro funk, funk and Chicago house feed in; minimal techno, acid, trance, IDM and Berlin club culture come out. Techno and house share roots but part ways, one leaning on song, the other on the machine.

Common misunderstandings

Techno is not another word for dance music; its drum pattern and timbre are specific. Nor is it unemotional, since its coldness is funk filtered through machines. Calling it a European invention misses Detroit, where deindustrialisation in the 1980s formed its backdrop, and the European habit of using techno for the whole dance scene in the 1990s drifted far from the original sense.
