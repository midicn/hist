Niccolò Paganini

The Genoese who pushed the violin to its limit and became Romanticism's first star

1 min read0 characters条目ComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

composerromanticitalyviolin

Life

Niccolò Paganini (1782–1840) was born in Genoa. He practised under his father's eye as a boy, played across Italy as a young man, and from 1828 toured Vienna, Paris and London, becoming the most sensational instrumentalist in Europe. His gaunt figure and uncanny fingers bred rumours that he had signed a pact with the devil; his health declined and he died in Nice.

His place

He stands at the threshold of Romanticism. His works were written almost entirely for his own instrument and are few: twenty-four caprices, six violin concertos, several sets of variations. The Twenty-Four Caprices, published in Milan in 1820, turned a solo violin into a small orchestra and made the performer, for the first time, a star independent of the composer.

What to hear

Listen first for the left hand: pizzicato, flying fingers, wide leaps — effects he produced on stage. Then double stops and chords: one violin singing two parts, like two people in conversation. Next, harmonics: bright, hollow tones that became Romanticism's image of the unearthly. Finally, the singing slow movement: do not remember him only for speed; in slow music he knew exactly how to breathe.

Key works

  • Twenty-Four Caprices, Op. 1
  • Violin Concertos No. 1 and No. 2
  • Il carnevale di Venezia, Op. 10
  • Variations on Moses

Influence and misreadings

Liszt, Schumann, Brahms and Rachmaninoff all took themes from him, and his technique became studies, variations and rhapsodies. He turned the violin's limit into the starting point for everyone who followed.
