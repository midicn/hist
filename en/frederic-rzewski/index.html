Frederic Rzewski

American pianist-composer who turned folk and protest songs into large variation sets

2 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-30

composercontemporaryamericapiano

A life in brief

Frederic Rzewski (1938–2021) was born in Westfield, Massachusetts, and studied composition at Harvard and Princeton with Sessions and Babbitt. In the 1960s he went to Italy and in 1966 founded Musica Elettronica Viva with Alvin Curran and Richard Teitelbaum, making collective music with improvisation and electronics. He then lived in Europe and taught in Belgium; he died in 2021.

The city he worked in

He was one of the few avant-garde composers to put politics into the notes. From the 1970s he turned folk songs, strike songs and revolutionary songs into large variation sets, most famously thirty-six variations on The People United Will Never Be Defeated. In form he continues the variation tradition of Bach and Beethoven, while his material comes from the street. He also keeps freedom, leaving order and duration to the player in many sections. His way of playing shaped the writing; much of the difficulty comes from the bodily experience of counting while playing.

What to hear

Listen first for how the theme survives. However far the variations travel, the tune stays singable; in Winnsboro Cotton Mill Blues follow when the song appears and when the machine swallows it. Then the use of the body, since he asks for forearm clusters, slaps on the case and even shouting, and those actions become rhythm. Then long structure — The Road consists of hundreds of short pieces, held together by repetition and recollection. Finally the temperature of lament, whose slow passages never plead but thicken the harmony layer by layer.

Key works

  • Piano: Winnsboro Cotton Mill Blues, Songs of Insurrection, The Road, Mayn Yingele
  • Variations: thirty-six variations on The People United Will Never Be Defeated
  • Other: Coming Together, Attica, chamber and improvised music

What remains

Rzewski made the piano a public instrument again — a place to meet, protest and sing rather than a stage for monologue. His work shaped later political composing and improvisation, and made pianists reconsider what their hands are for. He kept performing late in life, programming his own music alongside Bach and folk songs. He also wrote for ensembles and voices, but the piano stayed his public platform for fifty years.
