Mily Balakirev

Organiser of the Five, who turned folk song into a grammar for Russian music

2 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-30

composernationalismrussiapiano

A life in brief

Mily Balakirev (1837–1910) was born in Nizhny Novgorod and died in St Petersburg. He never attended a conservatory; he taught himself from Glinka's scores and from the church and folk singers he heard. In the 1860s he became the centre of the group known as the Five, with Cui, Mussorgsky, Borodin and Rimsky-Korsakov writing around him. With Lomakin he founded the Free Music School in 1862, conducted the Russian Musical Society concerts from 1867 to 1869, and published his collection of Russian folk songs in 1866.

The city he worked in

Balakirev was the first organiser of the Russian national school rather than its most prolific composer. He turned Glinka's example into a programme, insisting on folk song, oriental subjects and descriptive titles while still demanding Western formal training. His own output is small and endlessly revised: Islamey was first published in 1869 and reworked until 1902. In later years he turned to religious music and editorial work and appeared in public less and less.

What to hear

Listen first for the frame of the folk song. His folk-song collection does not harmonise the tune; it keeps the mode, the metre and the places where the singer breathes, which is why it can sound harder than a drawing-room song. Then hear the oriental rhythm. Islamey sets a Caucasian dance theme against perpetual-motion figuration, and the right hand has to stay as even as a drum before the middle section suddenly slows. Finally hear the patience of variation. The Grand Fantasy on Russian Folksongs Op. 4 pushes one tune from plain to elaborate, retexturing the theme at every return.

Key works

  • Piano: Islamey Op. 18, Dumka, Grand Fantasy on Russian Folksongs Op. 4, Sonata Op. 5
  • Orchestral: Tamara, two symphonies, Overture on Russian Themes
  • Vocal and transcriptions: songs, piano arrangement of Glinka's The Lark

What remains

His influence as a teacher and organiser outweighed his own works. Rimsky-Korsakov called him his true teacher in his memoirs, and Mussorgsky's early pieces were tried out in his drawing room. He gave the whole Russian school the idea that folk song is not raw material but grammar, an idea still audible when Stravinsky handles folk tunes.
