The Origins of Chinese Popular Music

Shanghai shidaiqu fused folk tunes, opera and jazz into the first Chinese pop idiom.

Related

ModernGong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, YuTrumpet

Time and place

Between the 1920s and 1940s, Shanghai's record companies, radio, ballrooms and film studios turned popular song into an industry. Li Jinhui's "Drizzle" (1927) is often called the first Chinese pop song. War in 1937 and 1949 pushed musicians to Hong Kong and Taiwan.

Musical features

Shidaiqu was a hybrid. Melodies drew on pentatonic folk tunes and urban shidiao; arrangements borrowed jazz syncopation, swing, saxophones and muted brass. Singers worked close to the microphone with conversational diction, and seven- or eight-piece dance bands played foxtrots, tangos and waltzes.

Singers and works

Li Jinhui wrote "Drizzle" and "Peach Blossom River". Zhou Xuan sang "The Wandering Songstress"; Yao Li sang "Rose, Rose, I Love You"; Li Xianglan sang Li Jinguang's "Night Fragrance". Chen Gexin and Yao Min supplied many melodies.

What to listen for

Hear the pentatonic skeleton, then the syncopated accompaniment. Zhou Xuan's diction matters more than volume; Yao Li's chorus works through repeated short phrases. The library has no 1930s recordings, so the examples are a melody sheet, a folk tune and a jazz-band texture, not historical sound.

Common misunderstandings

Shidaiqu was neither pure jazz nor pure folk song; it mixed the record industry, urban entertainment and regional tunes. Its lyrics were not only about love, since resistance songs were written too. Nor did Chinese pop begin in the 1980s: Shanghai had a star system thirty years earlier.
