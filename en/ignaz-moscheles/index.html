Ignaz Moscheles

A Prague-born pianist who made studies concert-worthy and taught Grieg

A life in brief

Ignaz Moscheles (1794–1870) was born in Prague, the son of a merchant. He studied piano at the Prague Conservatory with Dionys Weber, moved to Vienna in 1808, and took counterpoint with Albrechtsberger and composition with Salieri. He then toured Europe, settled in London in 1825 and taught at the Royal Academy of Music; in 1846 he moved to the Leipzig Conservatory at Mendelssohn's invitation, and he died in Leipzig in 1870. His diaries are an important source for Beethoven and Mendelssohn.

The city he worked in

He spans Classicism and Romanticism. He heard Beethoven as a young man in Vienna, played in London and Paris as an adult, and ended his career inside the Leipzig school. His studies are not mere finger drills but short pieces with themes and counterpoint. Between Czerny's purely technical volumes and the concert studies of Chopin and Liszt, he occupies a middle ground. Later players treated his studies as a bridge into Romantic piano language; Op. 70 and Op. 95 can be compared, one closer to the classroom, the other to the stage. He also wrote concertos, sonatas and many opera arrangements, but he is remembered mainly for the teaching music and the diaries. His output is large, yet the style stays clear and restrained, never relying on exaggerated effects.

What to hear

Listen first for the theme: each study in Op. 70 has a short motive, and the technical figure grows out of it rather than the other way round. Then for two lines: Op. 137b turns counterpoint practice into two-part piano writing, with imitation passing between the hands. Next, expression: in the Sonate mélancolique Op. 49 a lyrical theme and a dramatic passage alternate, like a person turning between two moods. Finally, bravura: the three Allegri di Bravura Op. 51 organise octaves, arpeggios and leaps into shaped paragraphs rather than mere speed.

Key works

  • Studies Op. 70, Op. 95, Op. 137b
  • Sonatas Op. 47, Op. 49
  • Hommage à Händel Op. 92, Hommage à Weber Op. 102, piano concertos

What remains

Moscheles pushed the study from finger gymnastics toward the themed character piece, the same road taken by the concert studies of Liszt and Chopin. He taught Grieg in Leipzig and carried London experience back to Germany. He gave the study both a technical target and a musical identity.
