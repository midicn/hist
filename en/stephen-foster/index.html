Stephen Foster

America's first great songwriter, whose tunes are instantly singable and whose history is not simple

Related

RomanticVoiceBanjo

Who he was

Stephen Foster (1826–1864) was born in Lawrenceville, Pennsylvania, now part of Pittsburgh. He had no formal training in composition and taught himself from the songs, hymns and Italian opera tunes he heard, publishing some two hundred songs in his lifetime. Oh! Susanna (1848) travelled with the gold rush, and Old Folks at Home (1851), My Old Kentucky Home (1853) and Beautiful Dreamer (1864) are still sung. He died in New York at thirty-seven, far from wealthy.

His place

Foster wrote for two places: the family parlour and the minstrel stage. Minstrelsy was the most popular American entertainment of the day, performed by white singers in blackface, its lyrics often in dialect and built on racial caricature; many of Foster's early songs were written for exactly that stage. He later wrote in a more respectful tone but never left the business. To hear his songs is to hear two things at once: melodic genius and the racist entertainment system that produced it. Neither cancels the other.

What to hear

Hear first how little the melody needs. Oh! Susanna moves almost entirely by step; a single leap lights up the phrase, and the chorus fixes it in memory by repetition. Then hear rhythmic character. Camptown Races uses a short accompaniment figure to imitate hooves; the verse narrates and the chorus bursts, and the faster it goes the more obvious the comic dialect becomes. Finally hear what arrangement changes. Old Folks at Home appears here as a theme and variations for piano: each return thickens the accompaniment and widens the register. Set beside a vocal recording, it shows which expressions belong to Foster and which were added later.

Key works

  • Songs: Oh! Susanna, Old Folks at Home, My Old Kentucky Home, Beautiful Dreamer
  • Others: Camptown Races, Hard Times Come Again No More, Jeanie with the Light Brown Hair

Influence

Foster's songs were arranged as dances, marches and instrumental pieces in his own century, then entered school books, films and popular music in the next. He proved that America could write songs from its own daily speech and settings without leaning on the European art-song tradition. Hearing him today also asks for historical awareness: the tunes are worth remembering, and the dialect and characters need to be put back into the minstrel context that shaped them.
