Brahms

A "conservative" revolutionary — Romantic feeling inside Classical forms

A life in brief

Johannes Brahms (1833–1897) was born in Hamburg and later settled in Vienna. He stands with Wagner as one of the two summits of the later nineteenth century: Wagner moved toward music drama and the future, Brahms returned to Classical absolute music — symphony, concerto, chamber music and variations.

The city he worked in

Brahms is often called a "conservative", but he does not copy the Classical: he uses Classical form as a vessel for Romantic density and feeling. His "developing variation" keeps transforming a short motive through a whole piece — a Classical developmental technique used as Romantic continuous narrative.

What to hear

Listen first for the growth of a motive: Brahms rarely repeats a theme literally; it is always changing. Then hear inner voices: his accompaniment is not background, often another melody. Finally hear restraint: he builds tension without titles or stage effects, inside absolute music.

Key works

  • Symphonies: four
  • Concertos: Violin Concerto in D, Piano Concerto No. 2 in B-flat
  • Chamber: Clarinet Quintet, Piano Quintet
  • Piano: Four Ballades Op. 10, Handel Variations

What remains

Brahms proved that Classical form could still carry modern feeling; his developing variation shaped Schoenberg and twentieth-century structural thinking. His disagreement with Wagner is one of the main axes of later nineteenth-century music history.
