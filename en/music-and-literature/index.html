Music and Literature

Literature gives music stories and sentences; music rewrites the speed at which we read.

1 min readMusic and CultureUpdated 2026-10-28

literatureart songtone poemnarrative

Related

RomanticLiedTone PoemVoice

Question

Literature gives music stories, characters, and sentences; music rewrites the speed of reading. Why does the same poem feel longer or shorter once it is set to music? How does music arrange breath, pause, and stress for words, and keep telling a story without them?

Historical background

Medieval minstrels sang epics, Renaissance madrigals split sonnets into voices, nineteenth-century art song brought poetry into the parlour, and the tone poem compressed novels and plays into orchestral narrative. Film and musical theatre kept drawing on it. Words provide the skeleton; music decides how fast it walks and where it stops.

A few examples

Schubert's Erlkönig sets a Goethe ballad for four characters sung by one voice, with piano triplets driving the horse. Gretchen am Spinnrade turns the spinning wheel into a piano figure, and the pauses in Goethe's lines become harmonic breaths. Mendelssohn wrote his Midsummer Night's Dream overture in 1826 and added the full incidental music sixteen years later, fixing Shakespeare's fairy world in woodwind and strings. The three show music as rearranging literary breath.

Listen to these

Listen to how the piano figure times the lines, and how the singer changes voice between characters. Literary music often turns at the end of a phrase rather than the beginning.
