MIDI 1.0

In 1983, synthesizers from different makers finally speak one language.

1 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-28

MIDIsynthesizerdigital-music1983

Related

ContemporaryMIDI ProtocolSynthesizerPolyphonic synthesis

The year it happened

In 1983 Dave Smith of Sequential Circuits and Ikutaro Kakehashi of Roland pushed manufacturers to accept a common digital interface; MIDI 1.0 was demonstrated at that year's NAMM show.

First, the event

Until then every maker's synthesizers, drum machines and computers spoke their own language, and even one cable depended on the brand. MIDI described which note, how hard, when on and off in a few simple messages, adding program change, clock sync and channels. Instruments from different makers could be chained, one keyboard could drive several modules, and a computer could store a performance as events rather than sound.

What was different after

Music production moved from tape to data: notes could be edited, quantized, copied and undone. Home studios, game scores, advertising and dance production became cheaper to enter, and synthesizers, samplers and drum machines formed a new kind of band. MIDI separated performance from timbre, so the same sequence could be replayed through any sound source.

Hear the turn

The MIDI Continuum puts MIDI in its title; the music is driven by event messages, not a recording. The second part switches timbre and rhythm through channel messages. The third uses triplets to create misalignment, proof that a grid can still breathe unevenly.
