Bedřich Smetana

Founder of Czech national music, with a river running through My Fatherland

1 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

Life

Bedřich Smetana (1824–1884) was born in Litomyšl in Bohemia, the son of a brewer. He learned the piano as a boy, went to Prague as a young man, ran a piano school and took part in the 1848 revolution. In 1856 he conducted in Gothenburg, returned to Prague in 1861 and later became chief conductor of the Provisional Theatre. He lost his hearing in 1874 and still completed My Fatherland in silence.

His place

Smetana founded the Czech national school. Bohemia then belonged to the Austrian Empire, and German dominated court and theatre, yet he insisted on writing operas in Czech and brought folk dance and national legend into the concert hall. His cycle of symphonic poems My Fatherland turns landscape, history and legend into six movements, of which Vltava is the best known.

What to hear

Listen first for the polka rhythm: the two-beat step has a homely spring, and its accents fall differently from a Viennese waltz. Then hear how a river is written: a melody begins softly, thickens, divides and rejoins, then flows away, storytelling through orchestration. Finally the rhythm of Czech speech: his opera melodies follow the accents of the language, so that characters seem to sing their words.

Key works

  • My Fatherland, a cycle of symphonic poems
  • Operas The Bartered Bride, Libuše
  • String Quartet No. 1 "From My Life", piano polkas

Influence and misreadings

Smetana opened the way for Dvořák and Janáček and helped make the national school a major route in nineteenth-century European music. My Fatherland still opens the Prague Spring festival. He turned a river into a nation's memory, and turned the silence of deafness into his loudest music.
