Jump Blues

The big band shrinks to six or seven players, a saxophone barks riffs and a singer shouts, and the floor fills

Jump blues is the blues of American city ballrooms from the late 1930s into the 1950s. It shrinks the big band to six or seven players, keeps saxophone, trumpet and rhythm section, and fills the floor with fast shuffle and shouted vocals.

Origins and era

Big bands shrank in the later 1940s, and players carried riffs and blues numbers into small clubs. Louis Jordan formed the Tympany Five in 1938, and Caldonia in 1945 and Choo Choo Ch'Boogie in 1946 made the format a national hit. On the West Coast Roy Brown, Amos Milburn and Charles Brown, and on the East Coast Wynonie Harris and Big Joe Turner, pushed jump blues onto the rhythm-and-blues charts. In the early 1950s it became one of rock and roll's direct sources.

What you hear

The band is usually tenor saxophone, trumpet, piano, electric guitar, bass and drums, sometimes a second sax. Drums play a shuffle with snare on two and four, bass plays one note per beat, and piano uses a boogie-woogie left hand. The saxophone plays short riffs and solos with a honking edge, the singer shouts, lyrics are comic and often in call-and-response. Tempos are quick, sections short, and the music is built for dancing.

Key figures

Louis Jordan joined the small band, comic lyrics and saxophone riffs, Big Joe Turner's voice could carry the whole group, and Wynonie Harris turned Good Rockin' Tonight into a hit. Amos Milburn and Charles Brown represent a softer West Coast side, while Tiny Bradshaw and Bull Moose Jackson left rougher records.

Lineage

Swing big bands, boogie-woogie and blues feed in, and rock and roll, rhythm and blues and soul come out. Bill Haley's cover of Shake, Rattle and Roll carried jump-blues repertoire to white teenage listeners.

Common misunderstandings

Jump blues is not rock and roll. It keeps the shuffle swing and jazz harmony, while rock moved the accent to two and four and sped up. Nor is it all fast, since slow blues ballads used the same band. Calling it merely a transitional style ignores that it was mainstream dance music in the 1940s.
