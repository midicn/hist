Leopold Godowsky

Pianist-composer who rewrote Chopin's studies as polyphonic labyrinths

2 min readComposersUpdated 2026-10-30

A life in brief

Leopold Godowsky (1870–1938) was born at Žasliai near Vilnius, in what was then the Russian Empire, and became a pianist, composer and teacher. His family moved to Germany while he was a child, he toured America as a teenager, taught in Chicago, returned to Berlin, directed a masterclass at the Vienna Academy from 1909, and spent his last years in New York, where he died in 1938.

The city he worked in

He belongs to the last generation of pianist-composers, for whom playing, arranging and writing were one trade. His reputation rests half on frightening technique and half on rewriting other people's music. The best known result is the fifty-three studies on Chopin's Études, some handing the right hand's part to the left, others adding a new contrapuntal line until a study becomes a three-part piece. After a trip to Java in 1922 he wrote the Java Suite, carrying gamelan scales and layering into the piano. He wrote original works too, and the Java Suite is the one to hear, since it stands on its own material rather than on borrowed tunes.

What to hear

Listen first for polyphony inside one hand. His arrangements often ask the left hand for melody, bass and accompaniment at once; check whether the parts really separate or smear. Then register transfer, where a phrase is moved an octave and changes from woodwind to brass colour. Then stacked rhythm: in the Java Suite several metres run together like bands sharing one hall. Finally the gap between surface and content — much of it sounds like salon waltz music until you see the page.

Key works

  • Piano: Java Suite, contrapuntal paraphrase of Invitation to the Dance, Three Concert Studies Op.11, Grand valse romantique
  • Transcriptions: fifty-three studies on Chopin's Études, left-hand arrangements
  • Other: Triakontameron, Passacaglia on a theme from Schubert's Unfinished

What remains

Godowsky pushed arrangement as composition to its limit and made left-hand technique a subject in its own right. Pianists who arrange, from Horowitz to present-day improvisers, cannot avoid him, and his Chopin studies remain both a technical test and a test of taste. His arrangements are often called excessive, yet that excess is exactly what shows where the limits of the piano lie.
