The Three-Minute Side

A record side held only three minutes, and that limit fixed how popular songs were written.

2 min readMilestonesUpdated 2026-10-30

music historyrecordsinglepopular song

A record side held about three minutes, and that limit reshaped how popular songs were written.

Year and city

New York, London and Chicago studios between 1900 and 1950. A 78 rpm shellac side ran three to four minutes; in 1949 the seven-inch 45 rpm appeared, similar in length, tougher in material and smaller across. Jukeboxes, radio slots and dance halls all treated a song as a separate unit, and engineers timed by the side, starting again when a take ran long.

What happened then

The studio worked by the side, so a song had to finish within a few minutes. Producers dropped the verse or reduced it to a two-line introduction, arrangers shortened the preamble and placed the hook at the start. The thirty-two-bar chorus became standard, with the singer adding improvisation or a key change on the last pass. Records had an A and a B side, radio played one side at a time, and jukeboxes charged by the side; a song was often recorded twice, once for dancing and once for broadcasting. Takes were timed and repeated until they fitted. The 45 inherited the length and made playing and carrying easier.

What changed after

The single became the unit of popular music, and songs were written around a repeated chorus rather than around development. Verse-and-refrain displaced the nineteenth-century strophic narrative, and performance was compressed into a peak within minutes. The LP later made the album the unit, but downloads and streaming restored the logic of the single, and pushed it further, so the first seconds must hold the finger.

Listen for this moment

Begin with Swanee, barely over two minutes, its chorus repeated as soon as it appears, with no development section. Then The Man I Love, a slow standard cut to fit one side, its feeling carried by harmonic colour rather than length. Finally My Bird of Paradise, a short theatre song with clean phrase ends, made to be played again on the gramophone. Before the turn an aria could take a recitative, a slow section and a fast one across a quarter of an hour; afterwards three minutes had to hold everything.
