Jean-Philippe Rameau

From harmony theorist to opera composer, the other pole of the French Baroque

1 min read0 characters条目ComposersUpdated 2026-10-28

Training and teachers

Jean-Philippe Rameau (1683–1764) was born in Dijon, where his father was an organist. He studied at a church school, spent years moving between posts as an organist, and studied harmony in parallel. He wrote his first opera at fifty, then became the central figure of the French stage for three decades.

His place

Rameau is the leading composer of the late French Baroque and one of the founders of modern harmonic theory. His Treatise on Harmony of 1722 explained chords through a fundamental bass, turning practice into a system. His operas and opéra-ballets continue Lully's tradition while adding denser harmony and sharper dramatic contrast; in the Querelle des Bouffons of the 1750s he became the emblem of the French line.

Where to start

Listen first for ornaments: Rameau writes trills, turns and arpeggios into the melody itself, not as decoration added on top. Then follow the bass: harmony is pushed forward step by step, and a modulation changes the colour of a whole passage. Finally hear the character of the dances: the same minuet can be tender or eccentric, and the title often tells the player how to stand.

Works and craft

  • Operas: Hippolyte et Aricie, Castor et Pollux
  • Opéra-ballet: Les Indes galantes
  • Harpsichord suites, the opera Platée

Influence and misreadings

Rameau's harmonic theory, tidied by later writers, became the basis of Classical harmony teaching; his operas proved that French could carry complex music drama. Berlioz and Debussy both rediscovered him. He put a theorist's mind and a stage composer's ear in the same person.
